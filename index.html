Shipping is a business where supply arrives three years late

Everything peculiar about the industry follows from that. Ships take years to build and trade for decades, so capacity is fixed when demand moves; freight rates sit near the cost of fuel for years and then multiply in months; and the people who do best are not the ones who run ships well but the ones who buy them at the right time. This guide starts at zero and follows the cargo — the fleet, the contracts, the rates, the box, the port, the chokepoints, the customs desk, the financing, the rules and the tricks — through a year in which one strait set the price of fuel and another is slowly reopening.

01 / Orientation

What a shipping business actually is

A machine that converts a fixed fleet of very large, very slow assets into a revenue stream priced, every day, by everyone else's trade.

Roughly four-fifths of world trade by volume moves by sea — on UNCTAD's count, somewhere above twelve billion tonnes a year of crude, ore, grain, coal, gas, cars and boxes. Almost none of it is visible to the people whose lives depend on it. A pair of trainers made in Vietnam, a tonne of Brazilian soybeans bound for Qingdao, and a cargo of Qatari LNG headed for Rotterdam all share one property: the cost of moving them is set in a market where supply is nearly fixed in the short run and demand is set by forces the shipowner does not control.

That is the organising fact of the industry. A ship takes two to four years to order, build and deliver, and then trades for twenty-five years. When demand rises, the only immediate responses are to sail faster, to wait less in port, or to route around fewer obstacles — and each of those runs out quickly. Once they do, freight rates go vertical, because the supply curve is vertical. When demand falls, the fleet does not shrink; it keeps arriving from the yards for years, and rates fall to the level at which the least efficient ship barely covers its fuel.

Everything else follows. Shipping is the closest thing in the real economy to a textbook perfectly competitive market, which is why it spends most of its time earning nothing and occasionally earns a decade's profits in eighteen months. Container lines, by most widely cited estimates, earned more in 2021–22 than across the previous several decades combined. The industry's defining skill is not running ships. It is timing the purchase and sale of them.

The spine of the business

Cargo owner → contract of carriage → forwarder or NVOCC → carrier → vessel (owned or chartered) → load port & terminal → chokepoint or canal → discharge port → customs → rail, barge or truck → warehouse → shelf

Two links on that chain carry most of the economics. The contract of carriage decides who bears which risk and which cost — the difference between a voyage charter, a time charter and a liner booking is the difference between owning the fuel bill and not. The chokepoint sits in the middle because a handful of waterways — Suez, Panama, Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb, Malacca — determine how many miles a cargo must travel, and miles, not tonnes, are what consume ship capacity.

Four things that make shipping unlike other industries

1

Supply arrives late and stays for decades

Capacity ordered in a boom is delivered into whatever market exists three years later, and then competes for a quarter-century. The orderbook is the most reliable forecast in the industry and the most ignored.

2

The asset moves to the price

A factory cannot relocate overnight. A ship can reposition to whichever ocean pays best within weeks. That mobility is why rates across regions converge, and why a disruption in one trade lane reprices all of them.

3

Distance is a form of demand

Close a canal and the same cargo needs more ship-days to deliver. Tonne-miles, not tonnes, absorb capacity — which is why wars and droughts have been the most profitable events in modern shipping.

4

The industry is offshore by design

Ships fly flags of convenience, are owned through one-ship companies, are taxed on tonnage rather than profit, and are insured by mutual clubs. The legal architecture is as distinctive as the economics, and it shapes every number you will read.

02 / The fleet

Four different businesses that happen to float

Container lines, dry bulk, tankers and gas carriers share an ocean and almost nothing else — not customers, not contracts, not cycles.

The phrase "the shipping industry" hides the most important distinction a newcomer can learn. Liner shipping runs scheduled services on fixed routes, like an airline, carrying many customers' cargo on every voyage. Tramp shipping — bulk carriers and tankers — goes wherever a single cargo needs to go, like a taxi, and is hired one voyage or one period at a time. The two have different economics, different contracts and different owners, and the containers on a liner and the iron ore in a Capesize respond to completely different demand signals.

Size is measured three ways and confusing them is a common error. Deadweight tonnage (DWT) is how much weight a ship can carry — cargo, fuel, stores — and is the unit of the bulk and tanker trades. TEU, the twenty-foot equivalent unit, measures container capacity; most boxes are actually forty-footers (FEUs, or two TEU). Gross and net tonnage are measures of internal volume, not weight, and they are what ports, canals and tax authorities charge on.

Liner

Scheduled, multi-customer, network business

A container line sells reliability on a fixed weekly rotation. Its costs are mostly fixed once a service is deployed, which is why liners fight for volume and why their profits swing so violently with the load factor.

Tramp

Single-cargo, voyage-by-voyage, spot business

A bulk owner sells a ship-day. There is no network effect and almost no brand: price is the only competitive variable, which is why the dry bulk market is the closest real-world approximation to perfect competition.

Industrial

Contracted, project-linked business

LNG carriers, shuttle tankers and many car carriers are hired for years against a specific project or customer. They trade more like infrastructure than shipping, with credit risk replacing freight risk.

03 / Contracts of carriage

Who pays for what

The charter party is the most important document in tramp shipping. It is, in effect, a precise allocation of every cost and every risk between owner and charterer.

A ship has three kinds of cost, and every contract in the industry is defined by which of them the owner keeps. Capital cost is the price of the ship itself — debt service and depreciation. Operating cost (OPEX) is crew, maintenance, insurance, stores and management: the cost of keeping a seaworthy ship ready to trade, typically $5,000–$10,000 a day for a bulk carrier and more for a tanker or gas carrier. Voyage cost is the cost of a particular trip: fuel (bunkers), port charges, canal tolls and agency fees. Fuel alone is usually the largest.

To compare a voyage charter with a time charter, the industry converts every voyage into a time charter equivalent (TCE): freight revenue minus voyage costs, divided by the number of days the voyage took. TCE is the universal unit of shipping performance. It is what an owner compares against OPEX to decide whether to trade or lay up, and what investors compare across companies. Laytime and demurrage govern the port end of a voyage charter. The charterer gets an agreed number of hours to load and discharge; beyond it, they pay the owner demurrage at a daily rate, and if they finish early the owner may pay them despatch. In container shipping the same words mean something different: demurrage is charged for a box left too long in the terminal, detention for a box kept too long outside it — a distinction that became a political issue after 2021.

The bill of lading is a document of title. This is the single most important legal concept in the trade. Whoever holds an original negotiable bill of lading is entitled to the cargo, which is why bills can be sold, pledged to banks as collateral for trade finance, and used to transfer ownership of a cargo several times while it is at sea. It is also why a forged or misdirected bill can make a cargo vanish — and why "delivery without production of the original bill" is one of the commonest sources of loss in commodity trading.
04 / How rates are made

The supply curve is a wall

Freight rates are set where a nearly fixed fleet meets a demand measured in tonne-miles. The result is a market that is flat for years and then violent.

Martin Stopford's Maritime Economics describes four markets that together make up the shipping cycle, and the framework is worth holding onto. The freight market sells ship services. The sale and purchase market trades second-hand ships between owners. The newbuilding market orders new ships from yards. The demolition market sells old ships for scrap, mainly to beaches in Bangladesh, India, Pakistan and Turkey. Cash moves between them in a recognisable sequence: high freight rates make owners rich, rich owners bid up second-hand prices, high second-hand prices make newbuilding look cheap, orders surge, the new ships arrive into a weaker market, rates fall, and old ships go to the breakers.

The supply side is the fleet adjusted for how it is used: fleet size, plus deliveries, minus scrapping, multiplied by productivity — speed, port time, the share of the fleet idle or in drydock, and the distance ships must sail empty. The demand side is not tonnes but tonne-miles: a tonne of crude moved from the US Gulf to China consumes roughly four times the ship capacity of a tonne moved from the North Sea to Rotterdam. Trade-route changes are therefore demand shocks. When Europe replaced Russian crude and products after 2022, cargoes that had travelled a few hundred miles began to travel several thousand, and tanker earnings rose for two years with almost no change in world oil consumption.

Because supply is so inelastic in the short run, small changes in utilisation produce large changes in price. Near full utilisation, a 2% swing in effective capacity can double or halve a rate. That is the mechanism behind every shipping spike: the Red Sea diversions of 2024, which added roughly 10–14 days to an Asia–Northern Europe voyage and absorbed a large slice of the global container fleet; the pandemic congestion of 2021, when ships waiting outside ports were effectively removed from service; and the fuel shock of 2026.

Consumption assumes 32 tonnes a day at 14 knots, scaling with the cube of speed, plus 3 tonnes a day in port. No weather margin, no off-hire, no EU ETS cost, no ballast bonus. Real voyages are optimised by operators with weather routing and fuel curves specific to the hull; the cube law is the right intuition and the wrong precision.

Slow steaming is the industry's main short-run lever, and the model shows why. Because fuel consumption rises roughly with the cube of speed, sailing at 12 knots rather than 14 burns about a third less fuel per day while taking only about 15% longer. When bunkers are expensive and freight cheap, the profit-maximising speed falls; when freight is expensive, owners speed up, adding effective capacity to the market. Slowing down also absorbs fleet capacity — an industry-wide speed cut of one knot can take several percent of effective supply out of the market without scrapping a ship, which is why carriers reach for it whenever rates soften.

Paper markets. Dry bulk and tanker freight are traded forward through forward freight agreements (FFAs) — cash-settled contracts on Baltic Exchange route assessments, cleared on SGX, EEX and CME. Owners sell forward to lock in earnings, charterers and traders buy to fix costs, and hedge funds trade them as a pure volatility asset. Container freight futures exist but remain thin. If you want to know what the market thinks rates will be next quarter, the FFA curve is a better guide than any analyst.
05 / Liner shipping

The box, the network and the alliance

Malcom McLean's container turned shipping from a craft into a network industry. The network, not the ship, is now the product.

In April 1956 the Ideal X, a converted tanker, carried 58 truck trailers from Newark to Houston. Before McLean, loading a general cargo ship by hand cost several dollars a tonne and took days; the standardised box cut the cost of handling to cents and the time to hours. Marc Levinson's The Box argues, persuasively, that containerisation did more to globalise manufacturing than any trade agreement. It made the cost of distance small enough that a supply chain could span three continents.

A container line runs services: a fixed rotation of ports — Shanghai, Ningbo, Yantian, Singapore, Rotterdam, Hamburg, Antwerp, back — with enough ships, typically ten to fourteen on Asia–Europe, to call each port once a week. Its costs are overwhelmingly fixed once the loop is deployed, and the marginal cost of one more box on a ship that is sailing anyway is close to zero. That cost structure produces the industry's behaviour: lines compete ferociously for load factor, cut prices toward marginal cost when ships run empty, and cancel sailings — blank sailings — to remove capacity when they cannot fill it.

No single carrier can serve every port pair weekly with its own ships, so carriers share vessels in alliances — vessel-sharing agreements that pool ships on a route while each line sells and prices its own slots. Since February 2025 the main east–west trades have been divided among the Gemini Cooperation (Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd), the Premier Alliance (ONE, HMM and Yang Ming), the Ocean Alliance (CMA CGM, COSCO with OOCL, and Evergreen), and MSC, which now operates largely on its own and is the largest carrier in the world by a wide margin.

Who books the box. Large importers — retailers, carmakers, electronics firms — are beneficial cargo owners (BCOs) and sign contracts directly with carriers. Everyone else goes through freight forwarders and NVOCCs (non-vessel-operating common carriers), which buy space wholesale from carriers, consolidate smaller shippers, issue their own bills of lading and handle customs, insurance and inland transport. The forwarding industry is consolidating fast: DSV's acquisition of DB Schenker, completed in 2025, created the largest forwarder alongside Kuehne+Nagel and DHL. Digital forwarders such as Flexport promised to disintermediate the business and have mostly ended up resembling it.

Container rates are reported by several competing indices — Drewry's World Container Index, the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI), the Freightos Baltic Index and Xeneta's contract and spot data — and they differ because they measure different things: spot versus short-term contract, all-in versus base, Shanghai versus wider China. When two indices disagree, it is usually because they are measuring different shippers.

06 / Ports and terminals

A port is a landlord; a terminal is a business

The public authority usually owns the land and the water. Private operators lease the quays and run the cranes — and increasingly, carriers own the operators.

Most large ports follow the landlord model. A public port authority owns the land, dredges the channel, builds breakwaters and roads, and leases terminal areas to private operators under concessions of twenty-five to fifty years. The operator buys the cranes, runs the yard, employs or contracts the labour and earns a fee per container moved. The authority earns rent, dues on ships and cargo, and the economic development that justified the port in the first place. Singapore, Rotterdam, Los Angeles, Long Beach and Antwerp-Bruges all broadly work this way; China's great ports are owned through state-controlled port groups.

Terminal operators are a concentrated, infrastructure-like business: PSA International (Singapore), Hutchison Ports, APM Terminals (owned by Maersk), DP World, COSCO Shipping Ports, China Merchants Port, Terminal Investment Limited (owned by MSC), ICTSI and Eurogate among them. The trend of the past decade is vertical integration: carriers buying terminals to guarantee berth windows for their own ships. MSC and Maersk now control large terminal networks and inland logistics businesses, which changes the economics of everyone who depends on those terminals.

What makes a terminal good

1

Berth productivity

Crane moves per hour per ship, and how many cranes can work one vessel at once. A 20,000 TEU ship exchanging 8,000 boxes needs to turn in a couple of days or the whole rotation slips.

2

Yard dwell time

How long a container sits before leaving. Long dwell clogs the yard, which slows the cranes, which delays the ships. Free time and demurrage charges exist to push boxes out.

3

Water and air draught

Channel depth and bridge clearance determine which ships can call at all. Ports dredge, raise bridges and build outer terminals to keep the largest ships.

4

Hinterland connection

Rail on the dock, truck gate throughput, barge links. A port is only as fast as the slowest way out of it.

Labour is the variable that newcomers underestimate. US East and Gulf Coast ports are worked under a master contract with the International Longshoremen's Association, which struck for three days in October 2024 and settled in 2025 on a six-year deal running to 2030 with sharp wage increases and restrictions on full automation. West Coast ports are covered by the ILWU through 2028. European and Asian ports have their own arrangements. Labour agreements set the pace of automation, and automation is the main source of productivity gains in mature terminals.

Port fees as policy. In October 2025 the United States began charging port-entry fees on Chinese-owned, Chinese-operated and Chinese-built vessels under a Section 301 action against China's shipbuilding dominance; China retaliated with fees on US-linked ships. Both were suspended within weeks as part of a US–China truce. The suspension runs through 9 November 2026; the US Treasury Secretary has said it will be extended to 10 January, and industry groups have pressed for longer. Watch for the formal notice — carriers have already been reshuffling Chinese-built ships away from US services to limit exposure.

07 / Chokepoints and canals

Geography as a cost line

A handful of narrow waterways decide how far the world's cargo must travel. Close one and you have added distance — which is to say, demand for ships.

The Red Sea crisis is the clearest modern lesson in tonne-mile economics. Houthi attacks on merchant shipping began in late 2023; by early 2024 nearly every major container line was routing around Africa. Suez Canal revenues fell by more than half. The diversion absorbed so much fleet capacity that it rescued container lines from what would otherwise have been a brutal overcapacity cycle — in 2024 they earned far more than anyone had forecast, from a war, on ships that had been ordered during the pandemic boom.

The return is the risk. When ships go back through Suez, the capacity that the Cape absorbed is released into the market at the same moment that record numbers of new ships are delivering. Analysts at Lloyd's List and elsewhere have argued that a full return could cut container rates sharply even without a demand downturn. That is the dynamic now visible on Asia–Europe, where rates have fallen for thirteen consecutive weeks as Suez transits rise — while the transpacific, which does not use Suez, stays elevated.

Distance is demand.

A canal closure does not reduce the cargo the world wants moved. It increases the number of ship-days needed to move it. Every major shipping boom of the past fifty years — the Suez closure of 1967–75, the 2008 China boom with its long-haul ore trades, the pandemic port congestion, the Red Sea diversion, the 2026 Hormuz fuel shock — was at bottom an event that absorbed capacity or added miles. Ask of any shipping thesis: what is happening to tonne-miles?

08 / Beyond the quay

The supply chain after the ship

The ocean leg is often the cheapest part of a door-to-door move per mile, and rarely the slowest part. Customs, rail, trucking and inventory decide the rest.

Incoterms: who owns the cargo, where

The International Chamber of Commerce's Incoterms define, in three letters, where risk and cost pass from seller to buyer. They do not transfer title — the sale contract does that — and they are the most common source of disputes over who should have insured what.

Customs

Classification and origin decide the duty

Every import is classified under the Harmonized System, ten digits in the US HTS. Classification, valuation and country of origin determine the duty; getting any of them wrong — deliberately or not — creates liability for the importer of record for years afterwards.

Customs

Bonded warehouses, FTZs and drawback

Goods can be stored duty-free in bonded warehouses or foreign-trade zones until they enter commerce, and duty paid on goods later re-exported can often be recovered through drawback. In a high-tariff world these are among the most valuable tools an importer has.

Inland

Rail, drayage and chassis

In the US, most long-haul container moves inland go by double-stack rail on Union Pacific, BNSF, CSX or Norfolk Southern — Union Pacific's proposed acquisition of Norfolk Southern would create the first coast-to-coast railroad and is under regulatory review. The short truck move from port to rail or warehouse, drayage, is the most expensive mile of the trip.

Inland

Warehousing and 3PLs

Third-party logistics providers run warehouses, fulfil orders and manage returns. Warehouse space near ports and population centres is a real-estate asset class in its own right.

Inventory

Just-in-time versus just-in-case

Lean inventories minimise working capital and are fragile. After 2021, many firms added safety stock and second sources; carrying inventory costs roughly 20–30% of its value a year once capital, storage, insurance and obsolescence are counted.

Inventory

The bullwhip effect

Small changes in consumer demand produce larger and larger swings in orders further up the chain. The 2021 import surge and the 2022–23 inventory glut were a textbook bullwhip, and container lines rode both ends of it.

The de minimis change. For years the US let shipments valued under $800 enter duty-free and with minimal data, which made direct-to-consumer cross-border e-commerce (Shein, Temu and thousands of smaller sellers) economically possible. The exemption was suspended for all countries in August 2025, and the 2025 budget law repeals the statutory exemption from 2027. Parcels that used to fly in duty-free now need entries, duties and brokers, which has pushed some of that trade back into containers and US warehouses.
09 / Ship finance and ownership

An industry of asset traders

Ships are expensive, mobile, long-lived and resaleable. That combination made shipping one of the world's great collateral markets — and one of its great speculative ones.

A new Capesize costs in the region of $70–75 million, a VLCC well over $120 million, a large LNG carrier more than $250 million, and a 24,000 TEU container ship over $250 million. Because a ship can be sold anywhere in the world and its value is observable from broker reports and recent sales, it makes unusually good collateral. Shipping finance has been built on that fact for centuries: banks lend against the ship, owners put up a slice of equity, and the ship's earnings service the debt.

The lenders have changed. European banks, once dominant, retreated after the 2008–2016 shipping crisis wiped out much of their portfolios. Chinese leasing companies — subsidiaries of ICBC, Bank of Communications, CMB and others — became the largest providers of ship finance, usually through sale-and-leaseback: the lessor buys the ship and bareboat-charters it back to the owner, who has an obligation or option to repurchase. Japanese investors provide tax-efficient operating leases (JOLCOs), Norwegian bond markets fund mid-sized listed owners, and export credit agencies back ships built in their own yards. Private equity and family offices come and go with the cycle.

The industry's most important owners are not listed. Greek shipowning families control the largest share of world tonnage by deadweight — roughly a sixth — ahead of Chinese and Japanese owners, with Singapore, Hong Kong, Germany, Norway and the US trailing. Each ship is typically owned by a separate single-purpose company, registered offshore, so that a casualty or a creditor claim against one vessel cannot reach the rest of the fleet.

Buying a ship: the asset play

Why owners are traders. Run the model with the defaults and then move resale from 80% to 110% of purchase price: the equity return can easily double. Move it to 50% and it can go negative even with healthy charter rates. This is why the best shipowners are remembered for when they bought — at the bottom, from distressed sellers and bankrupt banks — rather than how they operated. It is also why newbuilding orders cluster at the top of cycles, when owners are flush with cash and second-hand prices make new ships look cheap.

Shipbuilding is a three-country business. China builds over half of the world's ships by compensated gross tonnage and has won the majority of recent orders; South Korea (HD Hyundai, Samsung Heavy, Hanwha Ocean) dominates LNG carriers and high-specification ships; Japan holds most of the remainder. The United States builds well under 1% of oceangoing commercial tonnage, which is the background to the SHIPS for America Act proposals, the Section 301 port fees, and Korean shipbuilders' investments in US yards such as Hanwha's Philly Shipyard.

Insurance

Hull and machinery

Covers physical damage to the ship. Placed in the commercial market, much of it through Lloyd's and Nordic insurers.

Insurance

P&I clubs

Protection and indemnity: liability to third parties — crew injury, pollution, cargo damage, wreck removal. Provided by mutual clubs owned by shipowners; the twelve members of the International Group insure around 90% of ocean tonnage and reinsure each other.

Insurance

War risk

Excluded from ordinary cover and written separately for listed areas, often on seven days' notice of cancellation. When war-risk underwriters withdraw, a strait is effectively closed even if no one fires a shot — which is what happened in Hormuz in 2026.

Safety

Classification societies

DNV, ABS, Lloyd's Register, Bureau Veritas, ClassNK and others set technical standards and survey ships. A ship without class cannot be insured, financed or chartered by any reputable counterparty.

General average is the oldest rule in maritime law and still in daily use. When a master sacrifices cargo or incurs extraordinary expense to save the ship and the rest of the cargo — jettison, salvage, refloating — all parties to the voyage share the cost in proportion to their stake. After the container ship Dali struck Baltimore's Key Bridge in March 2024, its owner declared general average, meaning thousands of cargo owners with boxes on board had to post security before receiving their goods. Shippers who skip cargo insurance discover general average at the worst possible moment.
10 / Regulation, fuel and sanctions

The rules of an ocean nobody governs

Shipping is regulated by treaty through the International Maritime Organization, enforced by flag states and port states, and increasingly shaped by unilateral action from the EU, the US and China.

The IMO, a UN agency in London, writes the conventions: SOLAS on safety, MARPOL on pollution, STCW on crew training, the Maritime Labour Convention on seafarers' conditions. Flag states — the countries where ships are registered — are responsible for enforcing them on their ships. Because many flag states lack the means or will to do so, port state control fills the gap: inspectors in the ports a ship visits can detain it for deficiencies. Detention records, organised in regional regimes like the Paris and Tokyo MOUs, are a useful proxy for operator quality.

Alternative fuels are an investment decision being made under uncertainty. The risk for owners is buying a twenty-five-year asset optimised for a fuel that the regulation, or the price, later disfavours.

Sanctions and the shadow fleet. Since 2022, a fleet of several hundred ageing tankers with opaque ownership, minimal insurance and frequent flag changes has carried Russian, Iranian and Venezuelan oil outside Western price-cap and sanctions regimes. Ship-to-ship transfers at sea, switched-off AIS transponders and spoofed positions are routine. This fleet is a real pollution and safety risk, a growing target of Western enforcement, and a structural distortion in tanker supply: ships in the shadow trade are effectively unavailable to mainstream charterers.

11 / Tax, flags and tariffs

Where shipping profits are booked

Shipping pays very little income tax almost everywhere, by deliberate policy. Trade, meanwhile, has become the most heavily taxed it has been in ninety years.

1

Tonnage tax

Most major maritime nations — the UK, Greece, Norway, the Netherlands, Germany, Denmark, Singapore and others, and optionally the US for US-flag ships in foreign trade — tax shipping companies on the net tonnage of their fleet rather than on profit. The effective rate is usually a small fraction of 1% of earnings. Governments accept this because ships can reflag in a week and the alternative is losing the industry entirely.

2

Flags of convenience

Liberia, Panama and the Marshall Islands together register over 40% of world tonnage. Open registries offer low fees, no income tax, flexible crewing rules and fast service. The cost is reputational: some open registries have weak oversight, and flag-hopping is a hallmark of the shadow fleet.

3

Section 883 and reciprocity

The US exempts foreign corporations' international shipping income from US tax if their home country grants US companies an equivalent exemption, subject to ownership tests. This is why most listed foreign shipowners report almost no US tax despite carrying US trade.

4

Greek constitutional protection

Greek shipping's tax regime is protected in the country's constitution, a legacy of efforts to keep the fleet under the Greek flag and Greek management. It goes a long way toward explaining why Piraeus remains a global shipowning centre.

Tariffs: how they actually work

A tariff is paid by the importer of record to the customs authority when goods enter the country — not by the exporting country and not by the foreign manufacturer. Who ultimately bears the cost depends on bargaining power: suppliers may cut prices, importers may absorb margin, or consumers may pay more. In the US, duty is assessed on the transaction value of the goods, essentially the FOB price, excluding international freight; the EU assesses on CIF value, including freight and insurance. That difference means a freight spike raises EU duty bills but not US ones — a detail the landed-cost model above lets you test.

US tariff policy since 2025 has run on several statutes at once. Section 232 (national security) covers steel, aluminium, autos, copper, timber, pharmaceuticals and others; Section 301 (unfair trade practices) covers much of China trade and the maritime fees; and the broad "reciprocal" tariffs were imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act until the Supreme Court held in February 2026 that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs. The administration has since leaned on the other statutes. For importers, the practical consequences are constant reclassification, origin engineering, and — for duties paid under a statute later struck down — the question of refunds.

Transhipment and origin fraud. When duties on one country are much higher than on its neighbours, goods start arriving from the neighbours. Some of that is genuine relocation of manufacturing; some is simple relabelling, or "substantial transformation" that is not substantial. US Customs has steadily raised penalties and enforcement for origin evasion, and the importer of record carries the liability even if a supplier or forwarder arranged the scheme. A suspiciously cheap DDP quote from a supplier is a warning, not a bargain.
12 / Tricks of the trade

The promotional playbook

Shipping promotion rarely lies about the present. It extrapolates the present — which in a cyclical industry is the same thing.

Equity

IPOs at the top of the cycle

Shipping companies list when rates are high, because that is when investors will pay for them. A prospectus built on trailing earnings at peak charter rates is a description of the cycle, not of the company. Look at when the fleet was bought and at what price.

Equity

"Fixed-rate charter coverage"

Long charters signed at peak rates look like secured income. They are only as good as the charterer's ability and willingness to pay when market rates halve — and charterers have repeatedly renegotiated or defaulted in downturns.

Valuation

NAV from broker valuations

Net asset value per share depends on desktop valuations of ships by brokers, which lag the market and are rarely tested by actual sales in a downturn. Ask how many comparable sales support the number.

Valuation

"Eco ships" premium

Newer, more fuel-efficient ships do earn more. The premium claimed in investor presentations often exceeds the premium charterers actually pay, and it erodes as the rest of the fleet catches up.

Supply

Ignoring the orderbook

Every bull case for rates has to survive the orderbook: ships already contracted and scheduled to deliver. Container orderbooks reached record levels in 2025, by most estimates around a third of the existing fleet. If a thesis does not mention it, it has not been stress-tested.

Supply

Counting disruption as demand

Diversions and congestion absorb capacity temporarily. Earnings from a closed canal are real, but valuing a company on them assumes the canal stays closed. Strip disruption out and ask what is left.

Pricing

"All-in" quotes with surcharges outside

Freight quotes that look cheap often exclude BAF, PSS, war risk, terminal and documentation charges. Compare total landed cost, not base rate.

Pricing

Detention as a profit centre

In 2021 some carriers and terminals billed detention and demurrage when boxes could not be returned because terminals refused them. US regulators tightened the rules; the incentive did not disappear. Audit every D&D invoice against free time and terminal conditions.

Service

Rolled bookings and "guaranteed space"

In a tight market, cargo booked and confirmed can be rolled to a later sailing. Premium "guaranteed" products exist; read what is actually guaranteed and what the remedy is.

Compliance

DDP from unknown suppliers

A supplier who offers to deliver duty-paid at a surprisingly low price may be under-declaring value or misdeclaring origin. The US importer can still be liable. Insist on being importer of record, or on seeing the entry documents.

Data

Index shopping

Different freight indices measure different things. A company or forwarder citing whichever index flatters its argument this week is telling you about its argument, not the market.

Ops

Shadow-fleet exposure

Cheap tankers, unclear owners, P&I from unknown insurers and recent flag changes are signs of a sanctions-evasion vessel. Chartering one, or lending against one, carries legal and reputational risk far above any rate saving.

Two questions that do most of the work

What is the orderbook relative to the fleet, and when does it deliver? Almost every collapse in shipping earnings was visible in the orderbook two to three years beforehand.

What happens to tonne-miles if the disruption ends? Separate the earnings that come from the trade from the earnings that come from the trade being forced to travel further.

13 / Industry structure

Who's who

A highly concentrated container business, a fragmented tramp business, and a ring of financiers, insurers, brokers and builders around both.

Liners

The container carriers

MSC (by far the largest, around a fifth of global capacity), Maersk, CMA CGM, COSCO (with OOCL), Hapag-Lloyd, ONE, Evergreen, HMM, Yang Ming, ZIM. The top ten control the large majority of capacity. Several have diversified into terminals, airfreight, rail and logistics, with Maersk and CMA CGM the most aggressive.

Tramp

Bulk and tanker owners

Listed: Frontline, CMB.Tech (which absorbed Euronav and Golden Ocean), Scorpio Tankers, International Seaways, Hafnia, DHT, Star Bulk, Genco, Teekay, Navios. Private: hundreds of Greek, Norwegian, Chinese and Japanese owners, plus Japanese majors NYK, MOL and K Line across segments.

Industrial

Gas and energy shipping

LNG carrier owners such as Golar, Flex LNG, Nakilat, MOL and NYK, often with decades-long charters to project sponsors. Energy majors and traders control large chartered fleets.

Ports

Terminal operators

PSA, Hutchison Ports, APM Terminals, DP World, COSCO Shipping Ports, China Merchants Port, TIL (MSC), ICTSI, Eurogate. Hutchison's March 2025 agreement to sell most of its non-Chinese ports to a BlackRock–MSC consortium became entangled in US–China politics; check its status before relying on it.

Logistics

Forwarders and 3PLs

Kuehne+Nagel, DSV (now including DB Schenker), DHL, Expeditors, C.H. Robinson, Nippon Express, CEVA (CMA CGM), Flexport. Asset-light, working-capital-light, and paid on volume and margin per shipment.

Building

Shipyards

China State Shipbuilding Corporation and private Chinese yards; HD Hyundai, Samsung Heavy and Hanwha Ocean in Korea; Imabari and Japan Marine United in Japan; Fincantieri for cruise. Yards are the slowest-moving part of the supply response.

Money

Financiers and lessors

Chinese leasing arms of ICBC, BoCom, CMB and CDB; Japanese JOLCO investors; Nordic banks and the Oslo bond market; export credit agencies; private credit funds.

Market

Brokers and exchanges

Clarksons (the largest shipbroker and data provider), SSY, Braemar, Gibson. The Baltic Exchange, owned by Singapore Exchange, publishes the route assessments that settle freight derivatives.

Market

Data and intelligence

Clarksons Research, Drewry, Xeneta, Freightos, Kpler, Vortexa, Lloyd's List Intelligence, Alphaliner, Linerlytica. In shipping, information asymmetry is the business model of half the industry.

Rules

Regulators and institutions

The IMO; flag registries; port state control MOUs; the US Federal Maritime Commission (which oversees ocean carriers serving the US); the US Trade Representative; customs agencies; classification societies; the International Group of P&I Clubs.

14 / The present moment

Where the industry stands in October 2026

A fuel shock from one strait, a slow return through another, a record orderbook, and trade policy written statute by statute.

1. Hormuz made fuel the story

The US–Israeli strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026 effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz. For container shipping the direct effect was on Gulf ports; the larger effect was on fuel. Very low sulphur fuel oil rose by roughly two-thirds between mid-February and late May. Carriers responded with emergency bunker surcharges — Maersk sought US approval to apply one without the usual thirty days' notice — and quarterly fuel adjustments became the largest moving part of many freight invoices. Shippers rushed cargo forward ahead of the 1 July bunker resets, pulling peak season into June. Drewry's composite index rose from $1,958 per 40ft on 5 March to $4,639 on 9 July, its highest since September 2024.

2. Two oceans, two markets

Since July the indices have split. On Asia–Europe, rates have fallen for thirteen consecutive weeks: demand is soft and Suez transits are rising as carriers test the Red Sea again. Shanghai–Rotterdam was $3,337 on 8 October. On the transpacific, rates have climbed — Shanghai–New York went from about $7,900 in early July to $10,220 — on fuel costs, port congestion and Panama Canal draught limits that constrain US East Coast services. Carriers are trying to push FAK increases in the second half of October; Drewry has described their success as uncertain, and named a faster-than-expected Suez return as the main threat to carrier pricing.

3. The Suez return and the orderbook

The return to Suez is now gradual rather than hypothetical. Maersk recorded 67 canal transits in September against 8 a year earlier; CMA CGM never fully left; OOCL scheduled a trial voyage for October and Yang Ming is considering one. Every ship that stops sailing around Africa releases capacity, and it does so while a near-record orderbook delivers. Analysts have argued for over a year that a full Red Sea return would trigger a sharp fall in container rates; the Asia–Europe trade is the first place to watch that argument play out.

4. Trade policy by other means

The Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs removed the legal basis for the broad 2025 reciprocal tariffs; the administration has continued to impose duties through Sections 232 and 301, including a 100% Section 232 tariff on patented pharmaceuticals from 29 September. The Section 301 port fees on China-linked ships remain suspended through 9 November 2026, with an extension to 10 January announced by the Treasury Secretary and formal notice pending. China's reciprocal fees on US-linked ships track the US suspension.

5. What to watch

1

Asia–Europe rates vs Suez transits

The cleanest real-time test of the overcapacity thesis.

2

Bunker prices and Hormuz talks

Fuel is the marginal cost of every voyage; any settlement in the Gulf flows straight into surcharges.

3

November 9 to January 10

The Section 301 port-fee clock, and with it the deployment of Chinese-built tonnage on US services.

4

The IMO net-zero vote

Whether a global carbon price on shipping is adopted, delayed again or abandoned changes the economics of every ship ordered this decade.

Read every rate with its date attached. Container spot rates have moved from roughly $1,650 to $4,600 and back toward $4,350 per 40ft in twelve months. Any valuation, contract negotiation or budget built on a single rate is a bet on a number that changes weekly.
15 / Vocabulary

The words, defined

Filter by category or search. Shipping jargon is old, precise and occasionally means different things in different trades — demurrage being the classic case.

Tonne-mileEconomics

One tonne carried one nautical mile. The true measure of demand for ships: longer routes absorb more capacity for the same cargo.

Deadweight (DWT)Fleet

The total weight a ship can carry — cargo, fuel, water, stores. The size unit of bulk and tanker shipping.

TEU / FEUFleet

Twenty-foot and forty-foot equivalent units. Container ship capacity is quoted in TEU; most boxes are FEUs, and freight is usually quoted per FEU.

Gross and net tonnageFleet

Measures of a ship's internal volume, not weight. Ports, canals and tonnage-tax regimes charge on them.

CapesizeFleet

The largest common dry bulk carrier, around 180,000 DWT, carrying iron ore and coal. Too large for the old Panama Canal locks.

Panamax / KamsarmaxFleet

Bulk carriers of roughly 75,000–82,000 DWT, built to the old Panama Canal limits. The workhorse of the grain trade.

Supramax / HandysizeFleet

Smaller geared bulk carriers with their own cranes, able to serve ports without shore equipment.

VLCCFleet

Very large crude carrier: about 300,000 DWT, two million barrels. The standard ship for long-haul crude.

Suezmax / AframaxFleet

Medium crude tankers, around 150,000 and 80,000–120,000 DWT respectively.

MR / LRFleet

Medium range and long range product tankers that carry refined fuels.

PCTCFleet

Pure car and truck carrier — a floating car park. Capacity measured in car equivalent units (CEU).

Liner shippingFleet

Scheduled services on fixed routes carrying many shippers' cargo. Container shipping is the liner business.

Tramp shippingFleet

Ships hired for individual cargoes or periods, going wherever the cargo goes. Dry bulk and tankers.

Voyage charterContracts

Hire of a ship for one voyage at a price per tonne or lump sum. The owner pays fuel and port costs.

Time charterContracts

Hire of a ship for a period at a daily rate. The charterer directs the ship and pays fuel and port costs.

Bareboat charterContracts

Hire of the bare ship; the charterer crews, operates and insures it. Common in leasing and finance structures.

Contract of affreightmentContracts

A commitment to carry a volume of cargo over a period on ships of the owner's choosing.

Charter partyContracts

The contract between shipowner and charterer, usually on a standard form (GENCON, NYPE, ASBATANKVOY) with negotiated riders.

LaytimeContracts

The time allowed under a voyage charter to load and discharge before demurrage begins.

Demurrage (tramp)Contracts

Payment by the charterer to the owner for exceeding laytime, at a daily rate.

DespatchContracts

Payment by the owner to the charterer for finishing cargo operations early, usually half the demurrage rate.

Off-hireContracts

A period during a time charter when hire stops because the ship cannot perform — breakdown, deficiency, crew issues.

Bill of ladingContracts

Receipt for cargo, evidence of the contract of carriage, and — if negotiable — document of title. Whoever holds the original is entitled to the goods.

Sea waybillContracts

A non-negotiable alternative to a bill of lading, used when no transfer of title in transit is needed. Faster, and no document of title.

Letter of indemnityContracts

A guarantee given to a carrier to deliver cargo without the original bill of lading. Convenient, routine, and a common source of large losses.

General averageContracts

The maritime rule that all parties to a voyage share extraordinary sacrifices made to save it. Applied under the York-Antwerp Rules.

IncotermsContracts

ICC standard trade terms (FOB, CIF, DAP, DDP and others) that define where cost and risk pass from seller to buyer.

Importer of recordContracts

The party legally responsible for declaring goods, paying duty and meeting compliance obligations on import.

TCEEconomics

Time charter equivalent: voyage revenue minus voyage costs, divided by voyage days. The universal measure of shipping earnings.

OPEXEconomics

Daily operating cost of keeping a ship ready to trade: crew, maintenance, insurance, stores, management.

BunkersEconomics

Ship fuel. Usually the largest voyage cost. Very low sulphur fuel oil (VLSFO) has been the main grade since 2020.

Slow steamingEconomics

Deliberately sailing below design speed to save fuel. Also removes effective capacity from the market.

OrderbookEconomics

Ships contracted at yards but not yet delivered. The most important forward indicator of supply.

Scrapping / demolitionEconomics

Selling old ships to breakers for steel. Prices are quoted per light displacement tonne (LDT). The main way supply leaves the fleet.

Lay-upEconomics

Taking a ship out of service when rates do not cover operating costs.

Ballast legEconomics

A voyage without cargo, usually returning to a loading area. Unpaid, and a large share of tramp ship-days.

Baltic Dry IndexMarkets

A composite of Baltic Exchange dry bulk route assessments. Widely quoted as a global trade indicator; better read as a measure of bulk ship supply and demand.

WorldscaleMarkets

The tanker industry's freight rate system: rates are quoted as a percentage of a published flat rate for each route.

FFAMarkets

Forward freight agreement: a cash-settled derivative on a Baltic route assessment, used to hedge or speculate on freight rates.

SCFI / WCI / FBXMarkets

Container freight indices from the Shanghai Shipping Exchange, Drewry and Freightos-Baltic. They measure different things and often disagree.

Spot vs contractMarkets

Container shippers buy spot (weekly prices) or under service contracts (usually annual). The gap between them widens violently in shocks.

FAKMarkets

Freight all kinds: one rate per container regardless of commodity. The basis of most spot quotes.

GRIMarkets

General rate increase, announced by carriers. Often announced, less often achieved.

BAFMarkets

Bunker adjustment factor — the fuel surcharge on container freight, typically reset quarterly.

PSSMarkets

Peak season surcharge applied in the late-summer import peak.

Blank sailingMarkets

A cancelled scheduled voyage. Carriers use them to remove capacity and support rates.

Schedule reliabilityMarkets

The share of ships arriving on time. Collapsed in 2021, recovered, and now a competitive claim between alliances.

Alliance / VSALiner

Vessel-sharing agreements in which carriers pool ships on a route while selling separately. Gemini, Premier and Ocean are the main east–west alliances since 2025.

BCOLiner

Beneficial cargo owner — the shipper who actually owns the goods, as opposed to an intermediary.

NVOCCLiner

Non-vessel-operating common carrier: issues its own bills of lading and sells space it buys from carriers. Most forwarders operate as NVOCCs.

Freight forwarderLiner

Arranges transport on behalf of shippers, usually across modes, handling documents, customs and insurance.

TranshipmentLiner

Moving a container from one ship to another at a hub port, such as Singapore, Tanjung Pelepas or Algeciras.

Hub and spokeLiner

Network design in which large ships serve hubs and smaller feeder ships connect regional ports.

Demurrage and detention (container)Liner

Charges for keeping a container in the terminal (demurrage) or outside it (detention) beyond free time.

Free timeLiner

Days a shipper may keep a container without D&D charges. Negotiable, and worth negotiating.

ReeferLiner

Refrigerated container, powered on the ship and at the terminal. Higher revenue, higher cost, critical for food and pharma.

Landlord portPorts & land

A port authority that owns land and infrastructure and leases terminals to private operators.

ConcessionPorts & land

A long-term lease giving an operator the right to run a terminal, typically 25–50 years.

THCPorts & land

Terminal handling charge for moving a container between ship and yard.

Berth productivityPorts & land

Container moves per hour per ship. The core measure of terminal efficiency.

Dwell timePorts & land

How long a container stays in the terminal. Long dwell means a congested yard.

DrayagePorts & land

Short-haul trucking of containers between port, rail ramps and warehouses.

ChassisPorts & land

The wheeled frame a container sits on for road transport. Chassis shortages have stranded boxes at US ports.

IntermodalPorts & land

Moving the same container by more than one mode — ship, rail, truck — without unpacking it.

Draught (draft)Ports & land

How deep a ship sits in the water. Channel depth and canal water levels limit the maximum draught, and therefore the cargo.

Air draughtPorts & land

Height of a ship above the waterline. Bridges limit it, as in the Bayonne Bridge before it was raised.

ChokepointPorts & land

A narrow waterway through which a large share of trade must pass: Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb, Suez, Panama, Malacca, the Bosporus.

War risk premiumFinance & insurance

An additional insurance charge for transiting listed conflict areas, often a percentage of hull value per transit.

P&I clubFinance & insurance

Mutual insurer owned by shipowners, covering third-party liabilities. The International Group's twelve clubs insure most of the world fleet.

Hull and machineryFinance & insurance

Insurance against physical damage to the ship itself.

Classification societyFinance & insurance

Technical body that sets standards and surveys ships. Class is a precondition for insurance and finance.

Sale and leasebackFinance & insurance

The owner sells a ship to a lessor and charters it back, raising cash. The main product of Chinese leasing houses.

JOLCOFinance & insurance

Japanese operating lease with call option — a tax-efficient lease funded by Japanese investors.

Single-ship companyFinance & insurance

Ownership of each vessel through a separate legal entity, isolating liabilities.

NAV (shipping)Finance & insurance

Net asset value: broker valuations of the fleet minus net debt. Listed shipping companies trade at discounts or premiums to it through the cycle.

Newbuilding priceFinance & insurance

The contract price for a new ship from a yard, usually paid in instalments from contract to delivery.

Flag of convenienceTax & customs

Registration in an open registry with no requirement of national ownership, such as Liberia, Panama or the Marshall Islands.

Tonnage taxTax & customs

A tax regime based on a fleet's net tonnage rather than its profits. Standard in most maritime nations.

Section 883Tax & customs

US tax rule exempting foreign corporations' shipping income where their home country reciprocates.

Jones ActTax & customs

US law requiring cargo between US ports to move on US-built, US-owned, US-crewed ships.

Section 301 port feesTax & customs

US port-entry fees on China-linked and Chinese-built ships, imposed October 2025 and suspended under the US–China truce.

Harmonized SystemTax & customs

The international product classification for customs. The US uses a ten-digit HTS code.

Foreign-trade zoneTax & customs

A designated area treated as outside US customs territory, where duty can be deferred, reduced or avoided.

Duty drawbackTax & customs

Refund of duty paid on imported goods that are later exported or used to make exports.

De minimisTax & customs

The threshold below which imports enter without duty. The US $800 exemption was suspended in 2025.

Substantial transformationTax & customs

The test for whether processing in a country changes a good's origin. Central to tariff-evasion cases.

IMORegulation

International Maritime Organization: the UN agency that writes shipping's safety and environmental conventions.

MARPOL / SOLASRegulation

The main IMO conventions on pollution and safety of life at sea.

Port state controlRegulation

Inspection of foreign ships by the ports they visit, with the power to detain substandard ships.

IMO 2020Regulation

The global 0.5% sulphur cap on marine fuel, in force since January 2020.

ScrubberRegulation

Exhaust gas cleaning system that lets a ship burn high-sulphur fuel while meeting sulphur rules.

IMO carbon-intensity rating for ship operations and efficiency standard for existing ships.

The EU carbon market as applied to shipping, phased in from 2024 emissions.

EU regulation capping the greenhouse-gas intensity of ship energy from 2025.

Shadow fleetRegulation

Tankers with opaque ownership and minimal insurance used to move sanctioned oil.

Automatic Identification System: ship transponders that broadcast position. Switching it off or spoofing it is a sanctions red flag.

Moving cargo between ships at sea. Legitimate and routine — and the standard method for disguising sanctioned cargo origin.

16 / What to retain

Twelve things worth keeping

If everything else evaporates, these survive.

1

Supply arrives three years late

The orderbook is the most reliable forecast in shipping. Read it before anything else.

2

Tonne-miles, not tonnes

Distance is demand. Canal closures, sanctions and trade-route changes are demand shocks.

3

The supply curve is a wall

Near full utilisation, a small capacity change doubles or halves rates. Shipping is flat for years and then violent.

4

TCE is the common currency

Convert every voyage into dollars per day. Compare it with OPEX and capital cost.

5

Speed is a cost curve

Fuel rises with roughly the cube of speed. Slow steaming saves fuel and quietly removes capacity.

6

Who pays fuel decides who holds the risk

Voyage charter, time charter and bareboat are allocations of cost and risk. Read the contract.

7

The bill of lading is title

Whoever holds the original controls the cargo. Most fraud in the trade runs through it.

8

Owners are asset traders

Resale value drives returns as much as earnings. The best owners are remembered for when they bought.

9

Insurance can close a strait

When war-risk underwriters withdraw, a waterway closes without a shot.

10

Landed cost, not freight

Freight is one line among many. Duty, surcharges, handling, D&D and inventory decide the shelf price.

11

The importer pays the tariff

Duty is paid by the importer of record. Who bears it economically depends on bargaining power.

12

Disruption is not a business model

Earnings from closures and congestion are real and temporary. Value companies on what is left when the disruption ends.

The question to ask first, always

If every disruption in the world ended tomorrow, how much capacity would be released — and how much more is arriving from the yards over the next three years? Compare that with how much the trade is actually growing. Most shipping disappointments are visible in that single comparison long before they appear in the rates.
